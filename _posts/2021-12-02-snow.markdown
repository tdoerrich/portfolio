I joined **Poppermost Productions** as their **first dedicated Level Designer** on *SNOW*, an open-world snowboarding and skiing game. Before my arrival, a technical artist had placed assets to establish visual style, but gameplay design hadn't been formalized. My role was to define what made *SNOW* fun to play—designing areas that supported different riding styles while teaching players core mechanics through environmental design.

---

## Overview
**Position:** Level Designer (First hire in the role)  
**Platform:** PC (Steam), PS4 (PSN)  
**Engine:** CryEngine 3  
**Duration:** January 2014 - May 2016  
**Scope:** Designed 13+ unique gameplay areas, established level design workflows, implemented in-game events and goals system

**Key Achievements:**  
- Built level design approach from scratch for an open-world snow sports game
- Designed diverse areas supporting trick-focused, speed-focused, and technical riding styles  
- Established workflows and documentation standards for future iterations  
- Created event and goal systems integrated throughout the open world  
- Balanced player challenge and flow across urban, natural, and technical terrains

## Featured Areas

### The Shrine
![The Shrine](/assets/img/the-shrine.jpg)  

*Tight, technical area focusing on turning and precision control.*  

**Design Goals:**  
- Challenge new players while teaching braking and control mechanics  
- Encourage strategic line selection around natural rock obstacles  
- Create high-risk, high-reward gameplay for advanced players

**Approach:**  
Designed as a natural skill gate—players who tried to blast through at full speed would crash into rocks or fly off cliffs. Success required mastering speed control and reading terrain ahead.

---

### Eastwater River
![Eastwater River](/assets/img/eastwater-river.jpg)  

*Trick-focused descent with multiple rails and jumps crossing a mountain river.*  

**Design Goals:**  
- Offer multiple paths for freestyle exploration  
- Enable risk/reward gameplay through rails, gaps, and jumps  
- Support both conservative "safe" lines and aggressive trick lines

**Approach:**  
Created a natural corridor with the river as a central feature, providing clear direction while offering dozens of optional tricks. Players could ride conservatively down either bank or attempt risky rail-to-rail transfers across the water.

---

### Eastwater Village
![Eastwater Village](/assets/img/eastwater-village.jpg)  

*Urban environment for technical tricks and rail combos.*  

**Design Goals:**  
- Integrate realistic village architecture with gameplay flow  
- Encourage creative use of rails, ledges, and small jumps  
- Provide urban skiing contrast to natural mountain areas

**Approach:**  
Worked closely with art to ensure every building, fence, and structure could be ridden. Placed rails and ledges to reward creative line-linking while maintaining believable village layout.

---

### Eastwater Quarry
![Eastwater Quarry](/assets/img/eastwater-quarry.jpg)  

*Speed-focused downhill area with dramatic elevation changes.*  

**Design Goals:**  
- Teach players to balance speed, turning, and braking  
- Include mid-run obstacles to introduce strategic decision-making  
- Create pure flow experience for advanced riders

**Approach:**  
Designed long sight lines that rewarded speed while introducing periodic challenges (tight gaps, sudden turns) that punished pure speed without control. Multiple tiers allowed different difficulty routes.

## Systems & Workflows

### Level Design Approach
Established the core design principle that different areas should support different riding styles:
- **Trick areas** - Rails, gaps, and freestyle features  
- **Speed areas** - Long sight lines and flow-focused terrain  
- **Technical areas** - Tight spaces requiring precision control  

This segmentation allowed players to find their preferred style while encouraging them to explore different gameplay.

## Reflections

*SNOW* taught me the importance of **designing for player types, not just player skill**. Some riders want to hit massive jumps, others want to nail technical tricks, and others just want to carve perfect lines down groomed slopes. The best level design supports all of these without forcing players into one playstyle.

Working as the **first level designer** on a project also taught me to establish foundations carefully. The workflows, documentation standards, and design principles I created in the first few months shaped my work during the whole time there.
